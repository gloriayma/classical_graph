The movement divides into two main sections: measures 1-9 modulate from G-minor to D-minor, while measures 14-21 represent "an elaborated transposition, modulating from C-minor back to G-minor."

**Measures 1 & 14:** The bass transposes exactly, with identical intervallic structures featuring viiº6 with appoggiaturas.

**Measures 4 & 17:** Upper parts match with inverted soprano/alto voices. Importantly, the bass shows variance—Bach uses B instead of F-sharp "because that note is not playable on the violin, which has G as its lowest note."

**Measures 6 & 19:** Another discrepancy occurs where "the chord C/A/F# is awkward on the violin for the left hand. It also requires a shift up the neck, and the baroque violin was mostly played in the lower positions, usually first position."

Historical violin technique: early violins were lightly held rather than chin-supported, making downward shifts difficult. Composers like Bach accommodated this by "writing music in which downward shifts had many steps."

**Measures 7-8 & 20-21:** The bass lines differ fundamentally—the first descends stepwise toward the dominant, while the second "begins on the violin's lowest note and then goes up by step to the dominant." The passage uses "a striking dissonance with the crunch of a major 7th" and Neapolitan chords approaching the final cadence.

The final cadence differs significantly: measure 9 features "a simple octave double stop on the two inner strings" while measure 22 presents "a louder, arpeggiating across all the strings, and requiring a large movement with the bowing arm."
