I'd been wondering how long it would take for me to get around, in this more or less chronological amble through Monsieur S-S's works, to one of his genuine well known pieces. The pieces that appear on 'Best Of' compilations, or cheap '100 greatest hits of classical music' compilations (and actually even expensive ones). Well here we are at 1863, when Saint-Saens, at the age of 28 (surprisingly late for a child prodigy) wrote his Op. 28, the Introduction and Rondo Capriccioso for violin and orchestra in A minor.

As with his first and third violin concerto, and his Caprice Brilliant, this short concertante work was written for his friend, the well known composer-violinist and impressive moustache-bearer Pablo Sarasate. It runs for about 9 minutes and consists of slow introductory elegy, followed by a much more spectacular and downright balls-to-the-wall rondo, where the main tune is interspersed with lovely longing lyrical some other word beginning with L.

There are oodles of dissections of the structure and musical composition of this piece (a surprising number of professional violinist websites discuss it's ample technical difficulties in extraordinary detail), so I'll avoid a similar discussion here. But what I did want to talk about is whether, have listened to Saint-Saens's output up to this point, this represents a genuinely greater piece of work than those earlier works.

My feelings are still that this is one of a triptych of violin concertante pieces that Saint-Saens wrote that are genuine miniature masterpieces and deservedly far from obscurity (the other two being Danse Macabre, and the Havanaise). As to where this fits in compared to his output prior to this, I do think this shows a noticeable difference in inspiration levels, and more interesting in style. Saint-Saens is a very classically correct composer (far more, in the end, than was good for him in terms of his subsequent reputation with the likes of Debussy), with the criticism that his music, while nicely crafted, is often academically dry and unemotional. The IARC has way too much charisma and is far too enjoyable to fit that criticism. Why, Debussy even made a four-hand piano arrangement of this piece.

Quite what inspired Saint-Saens so particular here is not clear, but it's clear his intention was to write something fun for his mate Sarasate. It helps, I think that there is a distinctive Spanish flavour which helped draw S-S away from his more 'classically correct' style, but I also sense that by allowing himself to have some fun (as with the Tarantella Op. 6), Saint-Saens actually writes better music (perhaps the Carnival of the Animals is another good example of this).
