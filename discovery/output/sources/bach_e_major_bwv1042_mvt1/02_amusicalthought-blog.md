One of my previous flute teachers (who I cannot thank enough!) first introduced me to the wonders of Baroque music, she specialized in this style and I even once had the opportunity to play her baroque flute, nonetheless to say, I was very excited J I've been feeling a little nostalgic lately and so have been looking into some Baroque music and who else was to pop up but Bach. The third movement of Bach's violin concerto in E Major is a strong depiction of Baroque music and as I have been composing for violin I thought it could help give me some inspiration. The overarching characteristics of this piece clearly mark it as Baroque in origin; I mean the movement is heavily ornamented, decorated, with a clear set structure, use of figured bass and a definite exuberance to it. So baroque!

Structurally and harmonically this movement is fascinating. It clearly starts in the home key of E major, kicking off with the A section. This section lasts for a clear-cut 16 bars and has the solo and first violins doubling with a chordal accompaniment. Now, here is where things get interesting as Bach uses the B sections to modulate, through sequences and virtuosic movement in the solo violin line. You can see this at bar 25, where it moves towards F# major. I particularly like how the B sections are pulled about and take you on a journey through different keys and development before returning to the tonal center and stabilizing the piece within the A sections.

With an overall form of rondo, I've enjoyed listening to how the structure develops. You get the wholeness and feeling of roundness through the repetition of A section whereas the B sections add a sense of movement. Spina points this out, noting "each B section has virtuoso style writing but Bach uses different rhythmic devices for each section." Such is that the first B section has very little accompaniment, and the second has a more predominant accompaniment and a faster harmonic rhythm. The A section also develops towards the end where we see more chromaticism. Despite this, the piece is brought to a satisfying and complete close with a defining perfect cadence.
