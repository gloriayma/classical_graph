Dorothy DeLay, who taught at Juilliard School from 1948 until her death in 2002, shared eleven key insights about violin instruction during a 1995 discussion at the Joseph Joachim International Violin Competition.

DeLay emphasized that effective teaching demands significant time investment. She believed students naturally possess self-confidence and a sense of personal importance, qualities that education should reinforce rather than diminish.

Her pedagogical approach avoided direct criticism. Instead of telling students "that's wrong," she would say "that's fine" and then guide them toward self-discovery of errors. This gentle method prevented students from developing fear or withdrawal.

DeLay noted that "The audience arouses 'dragons' if passages haven't been practised properly." She encouraged students to move beyond past criticisms rather than becoming their own harshest judges.

Regarding talent, DeLay recognized multiple forms—verbal, mathematical, musical, spatial, physical, social, and moral—acknowledging that ability manifests differently across individuals.

On technical matters, she addressed shifting, describing the motion "like a train journey from A to B: before arriving at B, one gathers together one's things, gets up and disembarks, but without haste."

DeLay observed that vibrato should "support the line or phrase, so that at the end of the phrase, the vibrato relaxes." She also noted tension as a limiting factor in all aspects of violin playing.

Finally, DeLay stressed that teachers must help parents understand their children need relaxation and non-musical interests for healthy development.
