Bartók composed his two violin and piano sonatas in 1921–1922 for violinist Jelly d'Arányi. He frequently performed the Second Sonata with multiple distinguished violinists including Imre Waldbauer, József Szigeti, and Zoltán Székely. These works emerged during Bartók's "expressionist" period, alongside The Miraculous Mandarin and the First Piano Concerto, when his compositional approach aligned closely with the Second Viennese School of Schoenberg, Berg, and Webern. During American lectures in 1927–28, Bartók reflected: "There was a time when I thought I was approaching a species of twelve-tone music. Yet even in works of that era, fundamental tonal grounding remains evident."

Though Bartók labeled the Second Sonata "in C major," its tonality remains obscured. A tritone relationship between F-sharp and C becomes structurally significant. Unlike conventional sonatas, both works avoid traditional material-sharing between instruments. The Second Sonata departs from standard sonata form, adopting instead a condensed two-movement structure modeled on Hungarian verbunkos recruiting music: a slow section (lassù) followed by a fast section (friss).

The opening movement employs free, declamatory style with ornate figures and rhythmic flexibility characteristic of the lassù, rendered in dissonant language. Though abandoning complete Classical distance, it adopts a sonatina structure with exposition and recapitulation but no development. Bartók significantly varies his recapitulation's four expositional themes, creating a freely improvisational effect despite underlying organization. The opening theme recurs at the recapitulation's beginning, twice in the coda, and provides unifying force across both movements.

Octave displacement—placing successive melodic notes in different registers—appears, though less prominently than in the First Sonata's first movement. This technique substantially transforms thematic returns. Bartók's Hungarian Folk Music offers folk-based justification: "Hungarian peasants do not devote much care to selecting a suitable pitch, but they simplify difficulties in proportion as they occur: whenever a note is too high or low, they transpose by octave regardless of design and rhythmic conditions," creating characteristic seventh-interval leaps.

The connected second movement displays lively dance qualities resembling the friss section of verbunkos style, unfolding in rondo-like form with variously returning refrains. Bartók ingeniously connects both movements by recalling first-movement themes in intervening episodes and having the violin return to the opening theme near the climax. The sonata concludes with an atmospheric fade toward a radiant C major chord.
