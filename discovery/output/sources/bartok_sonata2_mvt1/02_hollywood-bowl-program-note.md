Béla Bartók's conservatory training emphasized German and Western European composers, but after encountering Hungarian folk music in 1903, he began exploring his native musical traditions. However, his Violin Sonata No. 2 transcends simple folk arrangement—it represents intricate modernist composition.

Bartók created an extraordinarily challenging work. He told his concert agent: "The violin part of the two violin sonatas… is extraordinarily difficult, and it is only a violinist of the top class who has any chance of learning them." Written in 1922 and premiered in Berlin the following year, the piece was dedicated to violinist Jelly d'Arányi.

The composer claimed C-major tonality, yet only the final chord confirms this harmonic center. The melodic language employs all twelve chromatic pitches rather than typical folk music's limited note palette. Bartók incorporates glissando and portamento techniques from Roma fiddling, exploiting the spaces between piano keys.

The opening features a solitary low piano note, followed by an improvisational violin gesture resembling a Romanian hora lunga. The two instruments function as sparring partners rather than soloist-accompaniment pairs. Classical form dissolves entirely; the violin occasionally plays without vibrato for an ethereal quality.

The second movement begins immediately with aggressive repeated piano rhythms answered by pizzicato violin, developing into a somewhat chaotic dance. The work oscillates between quiet introspection and stormy intensity, concluding with remarkable emotional restraint and expressivity.
