In early 1878, Tchaikovsky's patron, Nadezhda von Meck, gave the composer money for an extended holiday following his failed marriage and suicide attempt. Taking violinist Joseph Kotek as a companion, Tchaikovsky traveled to Clarens, Switzerland, where he read through violin music including Edouard Lalo's Symphonie espagnole. This work inspired Tchaikovsky to compose his own violin concerto, which he began on March 17 and completed in just two weeks.

The concerto initially faced significant criticism. Von Meck expressed personal displeasure, and Leopold Auer, the intended dedicatee and head of the St. Petersburg Conservatory's violin department, refused to perform it, deeming it "unsuitable to the character of the instrument."

The concerto's fortunes changed on December 4, 1881, when Adolf Brodsky premiered it in Vienna. Brodsky declared that "One can play the concerto again and again and never be bored," recognizing that repetition was essential for mastering its difficulties.

The work demanded innovative violin techniques that ultimately expanded the instrument's technical possibilities. Tchaikovsky explained his compositional approach: "I never compose in the abstract...I invent the musical idea and its instrumentation simultaneously."

The concerto's three movements showcase distinctive characteristics: an opening movement presenting lyrical themes that build in complexity; a brief canzonetta (small song) with simple ABA structure; and a finale featuring a rondo format with demanding virtuosic passages including scales, double stops, and rapid leaps.
