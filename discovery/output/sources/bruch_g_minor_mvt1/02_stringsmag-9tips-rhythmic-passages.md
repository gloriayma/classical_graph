The Bruch Concerto serves as a rite of passage for many intermediate violinists, introducing them to full Romantic-era playing. The piece flows beautifully until measure 18, where rapid, irregular rhythmic passages create significant technical challenges. Similar passages appear in measures 26, 31–32, 77, and 89.

1. Focus on Conceptualization: Discover the 'Why'

These measures contain rhythmic irregularities—groups of seven and ten notes, dotted rhythms, and 32nds—that would seem tame if written regularly. Understanding Bruch's intent illuminates how these groupings highlight virtuosic display, creating exciting lightning flashes of sound within the movement's alternation between lyrical singing and technical passages.

2. Channel Pablo Casals

Casals stated, "Without order there can be no freedom." Violinists must figure out rhythms slowly, using rhythmic goal posts. In measure 18, the septuplet on beat two divides naturally as 3 and 4 rather than 4 and 3. After speaking or singing the rhythm with a metronome at slow tempos, progression to violin playing should occur only "when you have built up to near tempo." Organize passages rhythmically through internal left-hand accents.

3. Shift Smoothly

Use open strings and rests strategically. Release shifts by starting with weight on the departure finger and releasing during the shift. This flowing motion—observed in great violinists—produces faster, more accurate position changes than abrupt, blocky movements.

4. Consider Impulse-Release

Within each passage's shape, identify impulse points for note groups. Accentuate these impulse notes with the left hand, then relax fingers on following notes. This approach creates musical flow while loosening the hand for shifting and finger action.

5. Don't Forget About Your Right Hand

The bow arm often receives overlooked attention in technical passages. Practice identifying where string crossings occur on specific beats. Slurred string crossings benefit from flowing arm movement following the bridge's curve rather than angular crossing, which disrupts arm weight transfer and tone quality.

6. Turn Up the Heat

Use a metronome to gradually increase tempo from achievable to slightly beyond achievable speeds. Continual envelope-pushing allows performance tempo to become comfortable through regular practice acceleration.

7. Don't Make It an Étude!

Incorporate dynamics, color, and vibrato from the beginning. Adding these musical elements later wastes practice time; fun and musicality should accompany technical work.

8. Practice Within Context

For each difficult passage, practice from at least one beat before and after, then from one full measure before and after. This contextual approach clarifies the musical purpose of each challenging section.

9. Use the Knowledge to Set You Free

After mastering individual technical actions, release conscious focus through breathing and body awareness. Shift attention to larger passage shapes and gestures rather than individual mechanics, allowing the Concerto's music to come alive.
